For most of my life, I have sought definite answers. I valued facts over theory, logic over thought, and explanations over the unknown. Over the past few years, that worldview has shifted.

I come from a religious family that practices Hinduism, one of the world's oldest religions. Yet I spent most of my life as a committed atheist, convinced that science held all the answers to life's origins.

During every prayer or religious ceremony in my youth, I felt out of place. I didn't understand the language or the stories. To me, it was all a waste of time.

In contrast, I am currently a devout believer in Hinduism. I believe that God will guide me in the correct direction and that I should always follow the will of God. 

So, when did my worldview change?

In October 2021, my grandmother passed away from a stroke at 88, after living a full and meaningful life. My parents and I traveled to Pleasanton, California to attend her funeral and say goodbye.

They told me how a Hindu funeral works. They explained that we all wear white clothing, we all say prayers and mantras for the deceased, and then we cremate them with various belongings we wish for them to have in their next life.

I loved my grandmother deeply and had countless cherished memories with her, but I resented the week-long trip for what I saw as merely a religious practice—a waste of time when I was just settling into my second month at Haverford, a place I was beginning to love.

The funeral was sad but beautiful. We said our goodbyes, then I went to my cousins' house while my parents remained to cremate her body. At the house, we lit a candle beside her photograph and played Ludo, her favorite board game.

The candle burned steadily for an hour as we played and laughed. Then my parents called—the cremation was beginning. On video, we watched the coffin slide into the furnace. I glanced at the candle. It was flickering violently, rocking back and forth in stark contrast to its earlier stillness.

For the first time, I wondered if God might be real. Science offered no explanation for the candle's sudden transformation at the exact moment the cremation began. This single moment cracked open my atheist certainties and began my journey toward Hinduism.

About eighteen months later, I underwent my Thread Ceremony—called a Poite in Bengali—a rite of passage for Brahmin boys.

I initially dreaded it. Three days in isolation on strict dietary restrictions with minimal entertainment. Two full days of prayers, many to be memorized beforehand. And I would have to shave my head completely.

Yet I entered with genuine openness. I decided to embrace the prayers rather than dismiss them. For the first time, I felt a true connection to the religion. Hearing the words translated into English helped me understand what I was actually participating in.

During my three-day retreat, I spent hours reading the Bhagavad Gita, the Song of God. One passage stopped me: "Man is made by his belief. As he believes, so he is. You are what you think you are. Your thoughts make and define you."

I had always believed in my limitless potential. Reading this crystallized my own convictions, yet I realized I had fundamentally misunderstood what Hinduism was about. Still, it wasn't quite enough to make me truly religious.

On the final day, as I prepared to leave the room where I'd spent three days in solitude, I felt relief and exhaustion and anticipation all at once. The last thing I wanted was to return to that isolation.

But solitude found me one more time. Alone again, I came across a line that would fundamentally alter my life. It revealed why atheism could never be for me.

"Perform every action with your heart fixed on the Supreme Lord."

I resonated deeply with this: human purpose transcends mere survival and reproduction—the materialist view that had never satisfied me.

This philosophy offered a contentment my atheism never provided. Though these principles defy scientific proof, I found them profoundly resonant.

When I returned, I committed to embracing Hinduism fully. Now, during religious ceremonies, I remain present in the moment, regardless of whether I understand every word.

I want to share two insights.

First, genuinely explore beliefs opposed to your own. I was unwavering in my atheism, but the moment I approached Hinduism with real curiosity, I discovered what had been missing from my worldview.

Second, engage fully in everything you do, even when resistance is strong. Had I approached prayer with genuine presence as a child, Hinduism might have revealed itself to me sooner. An open, engaged mind—especially when it feels unnecessary—changes everything.
