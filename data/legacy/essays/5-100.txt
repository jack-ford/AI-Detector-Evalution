Political debates serve as a window into the soul of a campaign, offering voters an unscripted look at the candidates who seek to lead them. The 2020 election cycle produced two such windows: a chaotic, deeply troubling presidential debate and a measured, substantive Senate debate that reminded viewers what civil political discourse can look like. Taken together, the two events paint a portrait of a nation wrestling with itself — one pulled toward spectacle, the other still capable of sober, serious conversation.
The first presidential debate between Donald Trump and Joe Biden, held September 29th in Cleveland, was widely regarded as one of the most embarrassing spectacles in modern American political history. Trump interrupted Biden over 70 times throughout the evening, making it nearly impossible for either candidate or the viewing public to follow a coherent argument. Moderator Chris Wallace repeatedly lost control of the exchange, at one point pleading with Trump directly to let his opponent finish speaking. The result was less a debate than a prolonged argument, driven more by contempt than by competing visions for the country.
"It was almost unwatchable," said one student, who described the debate as feeling like a schoolyard argument rather than a competition between two men seeking the most powerful office in the world. "Neither of them looked presidential. Trump was bulldozing everyone, and Biden kept telling Trump to 'shut up.' It was embarrassing for the country."
Biden was not without fault either. He struggled at several points to complete his thoughts clearly, trailing off mid-sentence and losing his footing on policy specifics. Critics on both sides acknowledged that the evening did little to inspire confidence in either candidate's fitness for office. There were moments where Biden appeared flustered and reactive, unable to pivot the conversation back to the substantive contrasts he had hoped to draw.
"Biden clearly wasn't sharp enough to handle the chaos Trump created," another student observed. "He had moments where you could see him lose his train of thought completely, which only gave Trump more ammunition."
Post-debate polling reflected the general disgust of the viewing public, with a majority of respondents in multiple surveys saying neither candidate had performed well and that the format itself had failed American voters. Many commentators called for sweeping reforms to future debate structures, including stricter enforcement of speaking time limits and the ability for moderators to cut microphones entirely.
Yet just weeks later, the Senate debate between incumbent Republican Thom Tillis and Democratic challenger Cal Cunningham in North Carolina offered a striking counterpoint. Though a state-level race, the debate drew national attention for its civility and depth. Both candidates outlined concrete policy positions on healthcare, economic recovery, and criminal justice reform. They disagreed sharply and passionately, but without the theatrics and personal attacks that had defined the presidential stage. Each man appeared to have prepared seriously, arriving with facts, figures, and a genuine willingness to engage.
"You could actually hear what they believed in," one student noted. "They were going after each other's records and ideas, not just trying to make the other person look stupid. It felt like an actual debate."
Another student echoed that sentiment, drawing a comparison to earlier eras of political discourse. "It reminded me of debates from ten or fifteen years ago, when candidates actually talked about policy. You walked away feeling like you understood both sides better, even if you still disagreed with one of them."
The contrast between the two debates raises a deeper and more urgent question about the direction of American political culture. Are the incivility and chaos of events like the first presidential debate an aberration, or have they become the expectation? The Senate debate suggested that respectful, issue-focused disagreement remains possible — that candidates can draw meaningful distinctions without resorting to interruption and insult. It proved that the problem is not the format of debates itself, but rather the willingness of participants to engage honestly within it.
Students engaged in civics and current events have much to learn from examining both debates side by side. The presidential debate is a cautionary tale about ego overtaking substance, a reminder of what is lost when political figures prioritize performance over policy. The Senate debate, by contrast, is a model worth aspiring to — proof that the democratic tradition of open, rigorous debate is still alive, even if it sometimes has to be searched for. Democracy functions best when its participants take it seriously, and the contrast between these two evenings made that truth impossible to ignore.
