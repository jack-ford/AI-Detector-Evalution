The Federation depot lay along the floor of a dry lake, a strip of baked clay nearly four miles long with the mountains closing in behind it like the walls of a trough. At the northern end, where a single road came down out of the pass, stood the headquarters block and the repair sheds, and beyond them the low domes of the tank farm, each one ringed by its own earth wall. Everything the fleet needed to stay in space for another year was under those domes.

South of the sheds the ground was given over to the ships. The cruisers came first, nineteen of them in two staggered lines, and after them the tenders and the fat-bellied transports, parked wherever there had been room. Kessel had studied them for three days through field glasses from the ridge, and they had seemed no bigger than fence posts. Now, crossing the clay in a dead major's tunic, he had to tip his head back to see where their hulls ended and the night began.

He had come through the wire behind the transports, which were dark and locked, and he kept to the lane between them as if he had business farther on. The clay had cracked into plates that shifted under his boots. Twice he caught his toe and had to turn the stumble into something like a brisk step.

Each cruiser had a man posted at the foot of its ramp. Most of them were smoking, or had wandered over to talk with the next man along. They straightened when they saw the braid on his sleeve, and Kessel returned their salutes without slowing.
