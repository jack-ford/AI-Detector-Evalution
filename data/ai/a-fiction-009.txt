The Staple Hall stood open to the evening, and through its great doors the lads could see the wool-factors still at their reckoning, though the bell of St. Nicholas had long since rung the close of market. Never had Hugh beheld so many nations gathered into one street. Flemings in broad hats chaffered with Lombards in velvet; a Scots pedlar cried his ribbons under the very nose of the town watch; and above them all, on the tall tower of the Hall, the cross of St. George stirred lazily in the wind from the sea.

It had flown there for two hundred years and more, since the third Edward starved the town into yielding, and no Englishman who looked upon it that night doubted that it would fly there for two hundred more. Alas for the pride of men! Before another winter was out the guns of the Duke of Guise would be heard across the frozen marshes, and the last foot of English ground in France would pass away for ever.

But of this the two cousins knew nothing, and they walked the ramparts with light hearts, pointing out to one another the ships in the haven and the distant glimmer of the French watch-fires.

"Enough, enough!" said Master Thornbury at last, coming upon them with his cloak already about his shoulders. "You have stared your fill, and the tide serves an hour before dawn. To bed, the pair of you."

They obeyed him, though unwillingly, and the hostess of the Three Crowns lighted them to a chamber under the eaves.

* * *

It wanted yet an hour of daybreak when the travellers came down to the quay, where a lantern swung at the stern of the hoy that was to carry them along the coast to Gravelines.
