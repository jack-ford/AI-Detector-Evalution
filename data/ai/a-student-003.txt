When a school assigns a project over the summer, it is asking students to keep learning during the one stretch of the year when nobody is watching them. That only works if students actually care about what they are doing. For that reason, I believe summer projects should be designed by students rather than by teachers. A project a student chooses is more likely to get finished, more likely to be done well, and more likely to teach something that lasts.

The first advantage is motivation. During the school year, a teacher can check in every day, answer questions, and remind the class about deadlines. In July, none of that exists. A student who has been handed a packet on a topic she finds dull will probably leave it until the last week of August and rush through it. A student who chose to build a model bridge, track the birds in his neighborhood, or interview a grandparent about immigrating to this country has a reason to start early, because the work is something he wanted to do anyway. Interest takes the place of supervision.

Student-designed projects also show teachers more about what each student knows. If thirty students complete the same worksheet, the teacher receives thirty versions of the same answers and learns very little about any individual. If those thirty students design their own projects, the teacher sees thirty different sets of strengths. One student may reveal a talent for writing, another for data, another for art. That information is useful at the start of a new year, when a teacher is still getting to know the class.

Some people worry that students will pick something too easy. That is a fair concern, but it has a simple solution. Teachers can set basic requirements, such as a minimum length, a connection to the subject, and a short proposal due before school ends. Within those limits, the choices should belong to the student.
