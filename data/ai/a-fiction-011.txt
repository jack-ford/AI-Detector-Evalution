So it was that Aldous Kray, who had never been farther from Earth than a campaign platform, stepped out of the shuttle lock at Farside Yard with the votes of nine hundred million people in his pocket. The Appropriations Board had given him its proxy without a murmur. The Assembly would pass whatever he recommended. One word from him and the half-built hull hanging over the crater would be cut up for scrap, and the forty years of work behind it written off as a rich man's folly.

He let himself enjoy the thought while the pressure equalized.

The inner hatch slid aside. Three people waited in the receiving bay: a gaunt man in gray coveralls, a younger woman with a clipboard held against her chest like a shield, and a technician who would not meet his eyes. Kray noted the technician with approval. Fear was a useful thing in subordinates.

"Commissioner," the gaunt man said. "I'm Lowell Tarrant, Yard Superintendent. You'll want to rest after the trip."

"I want nothing of the kind. The taxpayers did not send me here to rest, Mr. Tarrant."

"No," Tarrant said. "I don't suppose they did."

"They sent me to find out what a generation of their money has bought. I intend to see all of it. Not the parts you have polished for visitors."

Tarrant's jaw worked. He had heard the same speech on the newscasts for two years, almost word for word, and it had not improved by being delivered in person. The man himself did not frighten him much. The number of people who had listened did.

"We have nothing polished," he said. "We haven't had the budget."

Kray smiled thinly. "Then we shall get along. I shall require your records, a pressure suit, and someone to carry a light. Where I point it is my own affair."
