Captain had never belonged to anybody. A half-starved yearling, he had arrived at the Circle H as part of an auction lot and since then had carried whichever hand happened to need a mount. That was why, two mornings after Carol came, Nan brought him round to the porch with a blue ribbon plaited into his forelock. Fastened to the saddle horn was a card in her brother's untidy scrawl: "The property of Miss Carol Whitlow, and nobody else need apply."

Breakfast was long behind them. Nan chose the pace, and for most of the ride the girls spoke only to point out a hawk hovering above the draw or to remark how the sage had darkened after last week's rain. They kept to the fence along the north pasture, where Mr. Hollister's white-faced cattle raised their heads, stared, and returned to their grass, and they forded the creek beneath the abandoned line cabin. Yellow leaves were falling from the turned cottonwoods, drifting down upon them like slow snow. Past the water the ground climbed in broad swells toward the mesa. From the crest of the highest, the entire valley spread out behind the riders: the ranch house shrunk to the size of a matchbox, the windmill a silver thread at its side.

Nan reined in and rested her folded hands on the pommel.

"Once I believed I would die if I were kept here for good," she said. "About weekly I told Mother as much. Then I spent two winters in Boston, and nothing I saw there was half so fine as this. Tell me, Carol, is it just homesickness, or is it truly as lovely as it seems to me?"

Carol took a moment to reply. She was looking at her friend's sunburned face and thinking about the letters from Boston that had ceased in August, and about the care Nan had taken never to speak of them.
