The manager read the reference twice, holding it at arm's length as though it might improve with distance. Nell kept her eyes on the inkstand. She had composed the thing herself the night before on a sheet of Mrs. Tolliver's lavender notepaper, and in it a certain Miss Hattie Greer was praised as the most punctual, most honest and most uncomplaining waitress who had ever carried a tray in the state of Ohio.

"Says here you never broke a dish in two years," he remarked.

"No, sir," said Nell, who had broken three at breakfast.

"Well, we're short-handed, and the season opens Saturday." He pushed the paper back across the desk. "Six dollars a week and your meals. You'll room with the other girls over the laundry. Be here at half past five tomorrow, and ask for Mrs. Dunphy."

Nell thanked him in the small, grateful voice she had practiced, and got herself out of the office before her face could give her away. It had been almost too easy. Captain Welch had warned her that the Sea View never hired a stranger without writing to her last place, and she had spent half the night inventing a last place that could not be written to.

Now there was the matter of looking the part. Her good gray suit would never do, nor the hat. She walked down to the village and spent a dollar and eighty cents on two calico aprons, a pair of thick black stockings and a comb set with glass stones, which the clerk assured her was what all the girls were wearing.

The room over the laundry held four iron beds and a single cracked mirror. Three of the beds were occupied, in the sense that their owners sat on them, and all three owners stopped talking when Nell appeared in the doorway with her bundle.

"You the new one?" asked the tallest, without getting up.
