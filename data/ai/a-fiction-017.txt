Nobody had ever really owned Captain. He had come to the Circle H as a half-starved yearling in a lot bought at auction, and had been ridden since by whoever happened to need a horse. So on the second morning after Carol's arrival Nan had led him up to the porch with a blue ribbon braided into his forelock and a card tied to the saddle horn which read, in her brother's sprawling hand, "The property of Miss Carol Whitlow, and nobody else need apply."

They had been out since breakfast. Nan set the pace, and for a long time neither girl said much beyond a word about a hawk hanging over the draw or the color of the sage after last week's rain. They followed the wire along the north pasture, where Mr. Hollister's white-faced cattle lifted their heads to stare and then went back to grazing, and crossed the creek at the ford below the old line cabin. The cottonwoods there had turned, and the leaves came down around them like slow yellow snow. Beyond the creek the land rose in long swells toward the mesa, and when they reached the top of the highest one the whole valley lay open behind them, the ranch house no bigger than a match box and the windmill a silver thread beside it.

Nan pulled up and sat with her hands folded on the pommel.

"I used to think I'd die if I had to stay here all my life," she said. "I told Mother so about once a week. And then I spent two winters in Boston and never saw anything half so fine as this. Do you suppose that is only homesickness, Carol, or is it really as lovely as I think?"

Carol did not answer at once. She was studying her friend's sunburned face, and thinking of the letters with the Boston postmark that had stopped coming in August, and of how carefully Nan had avoided mentioning them.
