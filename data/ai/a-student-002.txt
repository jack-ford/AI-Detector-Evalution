In 1976, the Viking 1 orbiter was photographing the Cydonia region of Mars in search of a landing site for its sister craft when it captured something unexpected: a hill nearly two miles long that looked remarkably like a human face. The image spread quickly, and within a few years the "Face on Mars" had appeared in tabloids, books, and even a Hollywood film. Many people came to believe it was a monument built by an ancient civilization. As a scientist at NASA, I understand why the picture captured the public's imagination, but the evidence shows that the Face is a natural landform.

The original photograph was taken at low resolution, with each pixel covering roughly fifty meters of ground. At that scale, small details disappear, and shadows cast by the low sun happened to fall where eyes, a nose, and a mouth would be. Human brains are wired to find faces in random patterns, which is why we see them in clouds and electrical outlets. When Mars Global Surveyor returned to Cydonia in 1998 and again in 2001 with cameras ten times sharper, the "face" resolved into an ordinary mesa, much like the buttes found across the American West.
