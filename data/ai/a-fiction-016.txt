She came up the companion-ladder so quietly that I did not know she was on deck until I saw her shawl against the pale strip of sky over the taffrail. The calm had now lasted four days. The brig lay with her head all round the compass, the sails slatting against the masts at every heave of the swell, and the pitch soft in the seams even at that hour of the night.

"You ought to be asleep, Miss Harland," said I.

"It is too hot below, and my father is restless. Mr. Penrose, may I ask you something, and will you answer me truly?"

"If I can."

"The steward served out only half a pannikin of water to each of us this evening. He said it was your order."

"It was."

"Then there is less in the casks than you told my father yesterday."

I hesitated, for I had no wish to frighten her; but she stood looking at me so steadily that a falsehood would have been an insult. "Two of the casks in the fore-hold were started in the gale," I answered. "I only found it out this morning. We have enough for nine days at the present allowance, and a breeze must come before then."

"Must it?"

"In these latitudes a calm seldom holds above a week."

"That is not quite the same as 'must,'" said she, with a little smile that went to my heart, there was so much courage in it. "Thank you for telling me. I shall not speak of it to him."

She turned her face toward the sea, and I stood by her, wishing with all my soul that I had the wind in my gift.
