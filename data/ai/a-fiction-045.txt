For a while Monica made no reply. Pulling the petals off a dog-rose, she let them drift onto the towing-path one by one, while Mr. Ferrars looked on with the forbearance eleven years of teaching small boys had given him.

"I suppose you think I'm terribly ungrateful," she said finally.

"What I think is that you're very young," he answered. "That's another matter altogether, and nobody could hold it against you."

"People always say that when they want to be disagreeable." She tossed the bare stalk into the river. "Aunt Lavinia has said it twice since breakfast. I'm almost twenty. Since I was fifteen I have run Father's house, and I can't see why I should be posted off to Bournemouth like a parcel just because the doctor has decided I look pale."

"Your aunt worries about you."

"My aunt enjoys arranging people. Last spring she arranged the curate into marriage, and he's been in a daze since."

Mr. Ferrars's mouth quivered, yet he refused to laugh at his hostess's expense. Monica, realizing she hadn't shocked him, felt a sudden shame and went on walking without a word. Between its banks the river ran low, and past the willows the church clock gave five slow, cracked strokes.

"We'll miss tea," she said. "All afternoon I've been cross with you, and you only came out of kindness. I'm sorry. I'm not really the shrew I sound."

"I never thought you were."

"Then you're kinder than I deserve." A quick, rueful smile crossed her face. "I'll go to Bournemouth, and I'll even be polite about it, if you promise not to repeat to Aunt Lavinia what I said about the curate."

He gave his promise with great solemnity, and together they climbed through the orchard.
