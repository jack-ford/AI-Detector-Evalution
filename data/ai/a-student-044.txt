Americans head to the polls every four years convinced they are picking a president, when what they actually pick is a slate of electors whose votes are the ones that count. The Electoral College has settled every presidential race since the Constitution was ratified, but longevity is not proof that it works. A national popular vote should take its place, since the current system can give the win to whoever has fewer votes and gives candidates a reason to overlook most of the country.

Its biggest flaw is that the candidate with the most votes nationwide can still lose. In 2000 Al Gore collected about half a million more votes than George W. Bush across the country, but Bush won the presidency by taking Florida by a margin of only a few hundred. Nearly every state hands all its electors to the first-place finisher, so the size of a state victory is irrelevant: winning by a single vote pays off exactly as well as winning by a million. Millions of ballots therefore never change the outcome.

Winner-take-all also shapes how campaigns are run. Candidates know California and Texas will almost surely vote as they always do, so their time and money go into a few "swing states," Ohio and Florida among them.
