Picture a computer that notices a student's confusion before any hand goes up. The article "Making Mona Lisa Smile" says such technology exists today. Dr. Paul Ekman created the Facial Action Coding System, and Dr. Thomas Huang adapted it for computers, so software can now recognize human emotions by following the movements of facial muscles. Some people might feel uneasy about it, but reading students' expressions would be valuable in school, because lessons could then be adjusted to what each student needs.

According to the article, the system starts by constructing a three-dimensional model of the face containing all 44 major muscles. When one or more of these muscles moves, the movement is called an "action unit," and particular combinations of action units match the six basic emotions: happiness, surprise, anger, disgust, fear, and sadness. The software is accurate enough to have examined the Mona Lisa and reported that she is 83 percent happy, 9 percent disgusted, 6 percent fearful, and 2 percent angry. A computer that can pick out mixed feelings in a five-hundred-year-old painting can surely tell when a ninth grader is lost in algebra.

That matters because no teacher can watch every face at once. Among thirty students, a teacher could easily miss that someone in the back row tuned out ten minutes earlier. Dr. Huang notes that a computer in the classroom could tell when a student grows confused or bored and change the lesson accordingly, like a good human tutor. A frustrated student might get an easier explanation or one more example, and a bored one could move on to harder material. Rather than every student getting the same lesson at the same speed, each would receive teaching that responds to what he or she is truly feeling.
