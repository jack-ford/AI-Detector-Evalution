Neither of them said anything until the gate. He held it for her, and the latch, which had been broken since the spring, swung loose and knocked against the post.

"You ought to have that mended," she said.

"I ought to do a great many things."

They went down between the tamarisk hedges. The tide was out, and a boy was dragging a punt across the mud below the quay, stopping every few yards to rest.

"You said in your note there was something I should hear," he said.

"Yes." She pulled at the finger of her glove. "It's about the Trelawns' house. About Penhallow."

"Well?"

"My father dined at the Coppings' on Thursday. I wasn't there, so I have it second-hand, and father never listens properly after the port. But old Copping told him the place is to be sold. Not let. Sold, and before Christmas."

"That's nonsense. Henry would have written to me."

"Would he?"

He stopped walking. Below them the boy had got the punt to the water.

"There is a man down from Bristol," she went on, more quickly now. "A Mr. Garth. He has been twice to look at the roof, and he stayed at the Ship both times, and Mrs. Pascoe says he paid in sovereigns and asked a great deal about the fishing rights."
