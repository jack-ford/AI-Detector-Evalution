The article "Driverless Cars Are Coming" describes a future in which people summon a car, sit back, and let a computer handle the road. It is an appealing picture, and the author presents both the promise and the problems fairly. After weighing the two, however, I do not think driverless cars should be developed for public use yet. The technology is not ready, the legal questions have not been answered, and the supposed convenience is smaller than it first appears.

To begin with, the cars described in the article are not truly driverless. According to the author, the vehicles tested so far can steer, accelerate, and brake on their own, but they still alert the human when the road ahead requires skill, such as near construction zones or accidents. In other words, the car handles the easy parts and hands the hard parts back to a person who may not have been paying attention for the last twenty minutes. That seems more dangerous than ordinary driving, not less. A driver who has been watching the road the whole time is far better prepared to react than one who has just been startled by a vibrating seat.

The article also raises the question of responsibility. If a car driving itself hits another vehicle, who is at fault: the person sitting behind the wheel or the company that wrote the software? The author admits that the law has no clear answer, and that most states do not even allow these cars to be tested on public roads. Until lawmakers settle the matter, every owner of a driverless car would be taking on a risk nobody can measure.

Supporters argue that computers do not get tired, distracted, or angry, and that is true. Sensors can watch in every direction at once. Still, a sensor can fail, and a camera can be blinded by snow or glare. A human driver can at least recognize when something is wrong.
