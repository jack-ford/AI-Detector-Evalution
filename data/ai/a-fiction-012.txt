The bell rang for the sixth round before she had finished counting the bruises on his ribs. Tom came off his stool slowly, almost lazily, and Hagen crossed the whole width of the ring to meet him, as he had done at the start of every round, with his chin tucked down and his great red forearms working like the rods of an engine.

There was no sparring. Hagen drove his left into the body, and the sound of it carried to the back benches where Lottie sat with her hands knotted in her shawl. Tom gave ground. He gave it along the ropes, sliding sideways, his gloves high, taking on his arms and elbows what was meant for his head. Twice he was caught in a corner and twice he slipped out under the bigger man's swing, so close that Hagen's glove brushed his hair. The second time Hagen, carried round by his own weight, stumbled against the post, and a laugh went up from the miners in the gallery. He turned with his lips drawn back from his teeth.

It was then that Tom hit him. Lottie did not see the blow start. She saw only Hagen's head go back a few inches, sharply, as if someone had called his name from the rafters, and a thin line of blood run from his nose. The hall was up on its feet. The man beside her, a stranger with tobacco on his beard, seized her arm and shook it and bawled something into her face that she could not hear.

Hagen wiped his nose with the back of his glove, looked at it, and came on again. Nothing in him had changed. That was the thing she could not bear, that a blow like that should change nothing. He walked through Tom's left as a man walks through rain, and the heavy thudding at the body began once more, and she shut her eyes and listened to the rest of the round.
