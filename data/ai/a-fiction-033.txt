The bell I had heard was not for prayers, as I supposed, but for the bakehouse, and by the time I had found my boots a boy was already going from door to door with a basket of loaves. He handed me one through the window without being asked. It was still hot. I ate half of it standing on the porch, and then, being ashamed to idle while every chimney in the lane was smoking, I walked down toward the sound of hammering.

An old man sat on an upturned tub outside the wagon shed, mending a collar. He had a white beard cut square, and he did not get up, only shifted along to give me the dry end of the bench. I asked whether the shed was his.

"Mine?" He bit off the waxed thread. "It was, forty years gone. I had this and nine acres and a note at the bank in Millersburg that kept me awake nights. Now the shed belongs to the Colony and so do I, and I sleep. Ezra there has the heavy work. I do the stitching because my eyes are still good, and when they go I shall shell peas with the women and not think it any shame."

"And if a man will not work at all?"

"We had one such. He stayed a winter." The old man held the collar up to the light, squinting along the seam. "Nobody put him out. He got tired of eating alone."

I wanted to ask what he meant, but a team was being led up just then and he waved me off the bench.
