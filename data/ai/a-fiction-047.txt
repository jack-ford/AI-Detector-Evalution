So softly did she climb the companion-ladder that I never knew she had come on deck until her shawl showed dark against the pale band of sky above the taffrail. Four days the calm had held. Round and round the compass the brig swung her head, her canvas slapping the masts with each heave of the swell, and even at this hour of night the pitch lay soft in her seams.

"You should be asleep, Miss Harland," I said.

"Below it is too hot, and Father cannot rest. May I ask you something, Mr. Penrose, and will you give me a true answer?"

"If it is in my power."

"This evening the steward measured out only half a pannikin of water for each of us. He told me you had ordered it."

"I did."

"That means the casks hold less than you led my father to believe yesterday."

I paused. Frightening her was the last thing I wanted, yet she kept her eyes on me so steadily that to lie would have been to insult her. "Two casks in the fore-hold were stove in during the gale," I said. "Only this morning did I discover it. At the present ration we have water for nine days, and before then a breeze is bound to come."

"Bound to?"

"A calm in these latitudes rarely outlasts a week."

"That is hardly the same as 'bound to,'" she said, and the small smile that went with it touched me deeply, so much courage lay in it. "I am grateful that you told me. I won't mention it to him."

She looked away across the water. I stayed at her side, wishing with my whole heart that the wind were mine to give.
