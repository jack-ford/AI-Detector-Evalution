The prosecutor stood up and straightened his papers, which he did not really need because the Arbiter machines already had everything in there memory banks. "The state has shown," he said, "that Mrs. Kowalski ran a fortune telling booth without a license, and that she told at least nine customers the Arbiters would all break down by next year. This caused a lot of people to panic and some of them pulled their cases out of the automatic courts."

Judge Whitcomb leaned forward in his chair. He was the last human judge in the county and everybody knew it, including him. "Does the defendant have a lawyer?" he asked.

A small old woman in a green shawl got up. She looked more like somebodys grandmother then a criminal. "I don't want one, Your Honor," she said. Her voice was louder then you would expect. "I can talk for myself just fine."

The prosecutor kind of smirked at that. Judge Whitcomb saw it and he did not like it. He knew what the man was thinking, that this was going to be easy.

"Mrs. Kowalski, if you can't afford a lawyer the court can get you one," the Judge said.

"I got money. I just don't want one."

The Judge sighed. This was bad. If she made a fool of herself in his courtroom, the newspapers would say that human judges let crazy people waste everybodys time, and that a machine would of finished the case in four minutes. Which was probably true, and that made him even madder.

"She has the right, Your Honor," the prosecutor said, like he was explaining it to a little kid.

"I know what her rights are," Judge Whitcomb snapped. He wanted to say more but he stopped himself. "Go ahead and call your first witness."

But before the prosecutor could do it, the old woman raised her hand.
