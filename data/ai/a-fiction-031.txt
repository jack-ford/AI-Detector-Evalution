Lady Maud Penhallow (she had been a Miss Stubbs, of the Leeds Stubbses, and never mentioned it) discovered her soul in the spring of 1911, at a tea-party in Chester Square. A woman in a purple toque told her it was mauve. That settled the matter. By June she had turned the morning-room into what she called her Silence, with the blinds drawn and a brass bowl from Liberty's on the mantelpiece, and there she sat from eleven until a quarter to twelve, breathing through one nostril at a time while the footman turned callers away at the door. Her husband was not consulted. He was a large pink man who had made his money in linoleum and wished only to be let alone with the Financial Times; when she informed him at breakfast that his aura was the colour of mud he said, "Is it, my dear?" and helped himself to more kedgeree. She did not really mind his indifference, for it left her free to spend. She bought a crystal the size of a cricket ball. She bought eleven yards of orange silk to be made into a robe, and wore it once, and found it showed every crease. A Mr. Chandra Lal, who came from Bayswater on the Underground, was paid three guineas each Thursday to tell her about her previous lives, in all of which she had been of very good family, and he was given sandwiches afterwards in the housekeeper's room because Lady Maud had never been quite sure whether one asked him to stay to lunch.
