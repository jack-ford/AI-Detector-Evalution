"Buy the Sentinel? Lucy, that press hasn't been oiled since McKinley. Half the type is worn flat, the roof leaks over the paper stock, and old Pruitt owes the mill for six months of newsprint. Whoever takes the thing takes the debts along with it."

"Then offer him less," said Uncle Marcus, tapping his pipe against the fender. "He'd sell it for the price of a railway ticket to his sister's in Dayton. You've got your bonds at the bank, and that lot on Chestnut Street you never look at."

"And when those are gone? I've never set a line of type in my life. I can't write an editorial; I can hardly write a letter to my mother. We'd have to give up this house and board with the Hendersons, and you know what her coffee is like. No club, no motor trips, no new winter coat for you. Every merchant in town would smile at me across the counter and keep his advertising in his pocket. It isn't that I'm afraid of work. A fellow doesn't jump into the river to prove he can swim when he knows perfectly well he can't."

Lucy put down her sewing. "I can drink bad coffee. I drank it for two years at Miss Porter's and it did me no harm. The coat I have is good for another winter if I turn the collar. As for the merchants, I went to school with most of their wives." She smiled, though not very steadily. "I can keep books, Walter. I kept Father's for a whole summer. I'll fold papers and paste wrappers and write up the church suppers, and if the roof leaks I'll hold the bucket.
