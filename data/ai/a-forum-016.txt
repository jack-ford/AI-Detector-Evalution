If the person is struggling with a specific expense, paying that expense directly is often better than handing over cash. You can pay a utility bill, a month of rent to the landlord, a car repair invoice, or a medical bill without the money ever passing through their account. This guarantees the help goes where it is needed, and it is frequently easier for the recipient to accept, since it feels less like charity and more like a problem being taken care of. Grocery deliveries or a prepaid transit pass work the same way on a smaller scale.

Gifts, not loans

If you can afford it, give the money rather than lending it. Loans between friends and relatives have a way of souring the relationship: the borrower feels watched every time they buy something, and the lender quietly resents every late payment. A useful rule is never to lend an amount you could not afford to lose entirely. If you do decide on a loan, write down the amount, the repayment schedule and what happens if a payment is missed, and have both of you sign it. That sounds cold, but it prevents the far colder conversation two years later when each of you remembers the terms differently.

Paying down debt

For someone buried in high-interest debt, the most valuable dollar is the one that goes toward the card charging 25%. You could offer to pay off one balance in full on the condition that the card is closed or locked away, or pay off the balance yourself and let them repay you at zero interest. Either way, be honest with yourself about whether the debt came from a one-time emergency or from a habit. In the second case, paying it off without any change in behaviour just resets the clock, and covering a few sessions with a nonprofit credit counsellor may do more good than the payoff itself.
