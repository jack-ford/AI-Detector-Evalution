Every four years, Americans go to the polls believing they are choosing a president. In fact, they are choosing a group of electors who will cast the votes that count. This system, the Electoral College, has decided every presidential election since the Constitution was ratified, but that does not mean it works well. The Electoral College should be replaced with a national popular vote because it can hand victory to the candidate with fewer votes and because it encourages candidates to ignore most of the country.

The strongest argument against the Electoral College is that the winner of the popular vote can still lose the election. In 2000, Al Gore received about half a million more votes than George W. Bush nationwide, yet Bush became president after winning Florida by only a few hundred votes. Because nearly every state gives all of its electors to whoever finishes first, the margin of victory in a state does not matter. A candidate who wins a state by one vote gets the same reward as one who wins by a million. As a result, millions of votes have no effect on the final count.

The winner-take-all system also changes how candidates campaign. They know that states like California and Texas will almost certainly vote the same way they always do, so they spend their time and money in a handful of "swing states" such as Ohio and Florida.
