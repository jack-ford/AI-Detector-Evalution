Imagine a computer that could tell when a student was confused before the student ever raised a hand. According to the article "Making Mona Lisa Smile," this technology already exists. The Facial Action Coding System, developed by Dr. Paul Ekman and applied to computers by Dr. Thomas Huang, allows software to identify human emotions by tracking the movement of muscles in the face. Although some people may find the idea uncomfortable, using this technology to read students' emotional expressions would be valuable in a classroom because it would help lessons adapt to the needs of each student.

The article explains that the system begins by building a three-dimensional model of the face that includes all 44 major muscles. The movement of one or more of these muscles is called an "action unit," and different combinations of action units correspond to six basic emotions: happiness, surprise, anger, disgust, fear, and sadness. The software is precise enough that it was able to analyze the Mona Lisa and conclude that she is 83 percent happy, 9 percent disgusted, 6 percent fearful, and 2 percent angry. If a computer can detect mixed emotions in a painting that is five hundred years old, it can certainly notice when a ninth grader is lost during an algebra lesson.

This ability matters because teachers cannot watch every face at once. In a class of thirty students, a teacher may not see that someone in the back row stopped paying attention ten minutes ago. Dr. Huang points out that a classroom computer could recognize when a student is becoming confused or bored and then modify the lesson, much as a good human tutor would. A student who is frustrated could be given a simpler explanation or an extra example, while a student who is bored could be moved on to something more challenging. Instead of every student receiving the same lesson at the same speed, each one would get instruction that responds to how he or she is actually feeling.
