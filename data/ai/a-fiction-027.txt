Perkins counted the cups twice. There were two, and Mr. Abernethy was alone. He had been alone when he came into the dining-car at Poughkeepsie, alone when he ordered, and the waiter, a sour man with a napkin over his arm, had plainly thought so too, for he set the second cup down with a look.

"Odd," thought Perkins. "He hates tea. He told me so in March, at the Wheelers'."

He half rose to cross the aisle. Then he sat down again. A man who pours tea for an empty chair may not want company, and besides, Abernethy owed him eleven dollars and might think he had come about that.

So he watched. Mr. Abernethy put two lumps in the far cup, stirred it, and pushed it six inches across the cloth. He waited. He said something short and cross to the window, got no answer that Perkins could hear, and took one of the lumps out again with his fingers. After that he ate his chop very fast and without looking up.

It was at Yonkers that Perkins felt his collar grow tight. The train slowed, Abernethy stood, bowed stiffly to the chair, and went out past him without a nod. The far cup was empty.
