Our principal recently announced that every student must take part in at least one extracurricular activity. The goal behind this decision is a good one: clubs, sports and student government can teach teamwork and help students find new interests. However, making participation a requirement is a mistake, because it ignores the responsibilities many students already carry and takes away the very thing that makes these activities valuable.

To begin with, not every student has free time after the final bell. Some have part-time jobs that help their families pay bills. Others watch younger siblings until a parent gets home from work, or ride a bus that leaves ten minutes after school ends and have no other way home. For these students, a mandatory activity is not an opportunity. It is one more obligation competing with ones they cannot drop. A student who has to choose between soccer practice and a paycheck should not be punished for choosing the paycheck.

In addition, extracurricular activities work because the people in them want to be there. A debate team improves when its members care about winning arguments, and a band sounds better when its musicians practice because they love the music. If half the room joined only to satisfy a rule, the experience changes for everyone. Coaches and advisors would spend their energy managing students who are bored or resentful instead of helping the ones who are committed. The students who signed up by choice would end up with a weaker program than they had before.

A better approach would be for the school to encourage participation without forcing it, for example by holding an activities fair during lunch and offering clubs that meet during the school day.
