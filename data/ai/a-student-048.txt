Throughout the past century, owning a car has been thought of as an ordinary part of being an adult. Suburbs were built around driveways, shopping centers around parking lots, and whole cities around highways. Lately, though, communities in a number of countries have started trying out another notion: that life improves with fewer cars. The cases in the four passages indicate that cutting back on driving lowers pollution, eases stress, and makes cities nicer places to live.

Cleaner air is the most obvious gain. The first source says passenger cars produce 12 percent of greenhouse gas emissions in Europe, and as much as 50 percent in some car-dependent parts of the United States. Those figures turn real in the event the second source describes, when smog in Paris got so bad that officials kept half the city's cars off the road according to their license plate numbers. Traffic in the capital fell by 60 percent, and the air cleared enough within days for the ban to end. If one week of reduced driving can do that, a lasting reduction would accomplish much more.

Fewer cars also appear to make people happier. Vauban, Germany, is a suburb that mostly bans street parking and driveways, and 70 percent of its families own no car at all. A resident and mother of two told the reporter she had always felt tense with a car and was far happier without one. Hers is not an unusual experience. When traffic disappears, streets grow quieter and safer, children can walk or ride bikes to school, and shops are located within walking distance instead of out along a faraway highway. Bogotá, Colombia, has had comparable results from its yearly car-free day, which has since spread to other Colombian cities.
