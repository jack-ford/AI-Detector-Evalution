Evening had come, but the Staple Hall still stood open, and through its tall doors the boys could watch the wool-factors bent over their accounts, although the bell of St. Nicholas had rung the end of market long before. Hugh had never seen so many nations crowded into a single street. Broad-hatted Flemings haggled with Lombards in velvet, and a Scots pedlar bawled the praises of his ribbons right under the nose of the town watch. Over all of them, on the high tower of the Hall, the cross of St. George moved idly in the sea wind.

For two hundred years and more it had flown there, ever since the third Edward starved the town into surrender, and no Englishman who gazed up at it that night doubted it would fly for two hundred more. How vain are the hopes of men! Within a winter the Duke of Guise's guns would be booming over the frozen marshes, and England would lose its last foot of French soil forever.

The two cousins knew nothing of this. Light-hearted, they paced the ramparts, showing each other the ships in the haven and the faint glow of the French watch-fires in the distance.

"Enough, enough!" cried Master Thornbury, who had come upon them with his cloak already over his shoulders. "You have looked your fill, and the tide serves an hour before dawn. Off to bed, both of you."

Reluctantly they obeyed, and the hostess of the Three Crowns lit their way to a room beneath the eaves.

* * *

An hour still remained before daybreak when the travellers went down to the quay, where a lantern swung from the stern of the hoy that would take them along the coast to Gravelines.
