Monica did not answer at once. She stood pulling the petals from a dog-rose and letting them fall on the towing-path, and Mr. Ferrars watched her with the patience of a man who has taught small boys for eleven years.

"You think me very ungrateful," she said at last.

"I think you very young," he replied, "which is quite a different matter, and one for which nobody can blame you."

"That is what everybody says when they mean to be disagreeable." She flung the stalk into the water. "Aunt Lavinia has said it twice since breakfast. I am nearly twenty, and I have kept house for Father since I was fifteen, and I do not see why I should be sent to Bournemouth like a parcel because the doctor has taken it into his head that I look pale."

"Your aunt is anxious about you."

"My aunt likes arranging people. She arranged the curate into matrimony last spring, and he has looked dazed ever since."

Mr. Ferrars's mouth twitched, but he would not be drawn into laughing at his hostess, and Monica, seeing that she had failed to shock him, grew suddenly ashamed of herself and walked on in silence. The river ran very low between its banks, and beyond the willows the church clock struck five in a slow, cracked voice.

"We shall be late for tea," she said, "and I have been cross with you the whole afternoon, when you only came out of kindness. I am sorry. I am not really such a shrew as I sound."

"I never supposed you were."

"Then you are more charitable than I deserve." She gave him a quick, rueful smile. "I will go to Bournemouth. I will even be civil about it, if you will promise not to tell Aunt Lavinia what I said about the curate."

He promised gravely, and they went up through the orchard together.
