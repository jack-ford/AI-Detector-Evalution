"Then don't tell her," said Peg. "Not till Mother gets back Thursday. Miss Abbie would lock us in the woodshed if she thought there was a tramp sleeping in the icehouse, and I for one mean to go blueberrying tomorrow, tramp or no tramp."

"He isn't a tramp. Tramps don't wear spectacles."

"Some might."

Dot came down the back stairs just then with her hair still wet from the pump and one stocking on. She had been sent to wash off the worst of the henhouse, and she smelled of yellow soap. "Is it supper? I heard the bell."

"That was the Fosters' cow."

It was supper, though, twenty minutes later—cold ham and Miss Abbie's raised biscuits, and a dish of stewed gooseberries that nobody finished. By half past six the three of them were out the gate with their sweaters tied round their waists. Miss Abbie followed at her own pace, carrying a lantern she would not need for two hours yet.

The road to the landing ran downhill between stone walls. Somebody had been burning brush, and the smoke lay in the hollow like milk in a saucer. At the bottom they found the Tarbell boys bailing out a dory with a tin dipper and a boot.

"She leaks some," said the older one, Win. He did not look up. "But she'll hold five if you sit still."

"We need room for six," Peg told him. "Miss Abbie's coming."

Win looked up the hill, saw the lantern, and put down the boot. There was a scow tied under the alders that his father used for hauling salt hay. It was wide as a barn door and nearly as hard to steer. He and his brother dragged it round to the float without a word of complaint, and Miss Abbie stepped aboard with her skirts gathered in one fist. She sat down square in the middle on an upturned bucket.

"Well?" she said. "Push."
