For most of the last century, owning a car has been treated as a basic part of adult life. Suburbs were designed around driveways, shopping centers around parking lots, and entire cities around highways. Recently, however, communities in several countries have begun to test a different idea: that life can be better with fewer cars. The examples described in the four passages suggest that limiting car usage reduces pollution, lowers stress, and makes cities more pleasant places to live.

The clearest advantage is cleaner air. According to the first source, passenger cars are responsible for 12 percent of greenhouse gas emissions in Europe and as much as 50 percent in some car-heavy areas of the United States. Those numbers become real during events like the one described in the second source, when Paris experienced smog so severe that officials banned half the city's cars from the road based on their license plate numbers. Congestion in the capital dropped by 60 percent, and within days the air had cleared enough for the ban to be lifted. If a single week of reduced driving can produce that kind of change, a permanent reduction would do far more.

Limiting cars also seems to make people happier. In Vauban, Germany, a suburb where street parking and driveways are generally forbidden, 70 percent of families do not own a car at all. One resident, a mother of two, told the reporter that she had always been tense when she owned a car and was much happier without one. Her experience is not unusual. Without traffic, streets become quieter and safer, children can walk or bike to school, and stores are placed within walking distance instead of along a distant highway. Bogotá, Colombia, has seen similar results from its annual car-free day, which has since spread to other Colombian cities.
